\CourseSection{Readability Pilot and Supporting Detail}\label{app:readability-pilot}

We ran the method on the official 13,000-skill GitSkills sample to check feasibility and choose the preprocessing.
Of the 13,000 distinct skills, 237 were not named exactly \texttt{SKILL.md}, 1,566 had invalid front matter, 679 were too short (a body under 300 characters or fewer than 30 words of prose), and 2,049 failed a simple English check.
This left 8,469 skills from 5,653 repositories.
Figure~\ref{fig:readability-pilot}a shows why the Markdown must be parsed before scoring: on raw text, the median grade rises from 9.1 to 12.2.
Figure~\ref{fig:readability-pilot}b shows that skill sentences are short (a median of 9 words), but Coleman--Liau, which weights word length, places the same text near grade 13, which reflects technical vocabulary.
Descriptions read about 3.6 grades harder than bodies.
Keeping inline code as a one-word token instead of removing it lowers the median grade from 9.1 to 8.9. List-heavy skills did not read easier in our sample ($\rho = 0.19$ between list share and grade), although list-heavy files scored as easier in the context-file study~\cite{chatlatanagulchai2026agent}. The median Flesch Reading Ease of 46.9 falls within the 42.4 to 51.4 reported for context files. As a worked example, the body of Mozilla's bug-filing skill~\cite{mozilla2026bugfiling} grades at 8.1, and its 63-word description at 8.9, about four grades easier than the median description; 20 of its 30 text blocks are hard-wrapped.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{figures/readability-pilot.pdf}
\caption{Readability pilot on English skills from the 13,000-skill GitSkills sample. (a)~Flesch--Kincaid grade of the body prose, with the Markdown parsed and as raw text. (b)~Body grade under both formulas, and Flesch--Kincaid grade of the description. Boxes show the interquartile range, and whiskers extend to 1.5 times that range.}
\label{fig:readability-pilot}
\end{figure}

For code, 5,972 of the 8,469 skills contain a fenced block, 4,570 have at least one complete 10-line window of code, and 901 have a window of bundled script. Tokens came from an approximate language-neutral tokenizer and entropy from bytes, so scores are indicative. The median linear score was $-12.32$ for code blocks (interquartile range $-18.17$ to $-7.99$) and $-11.75$ for bundled scripts ($-14.89$ to $-9.13$), a negligible overall difference (Cliff's $\delta = -0.044$). Within the 586 skills having both, scripts scored higher in 65\% of cases, with a median difference of $2.65$, so the paired comparison will be reported as well. Applying the model's sigmoid would place 91\% of code-block skills and 98\% of script skills below 0.01, which motivates the linear output (DD-3). Because the score grows with line count, windows of other lengths are not comparable.
